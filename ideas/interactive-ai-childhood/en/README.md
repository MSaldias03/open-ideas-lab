# Interactive AI Childhood — Full proposal (EN)

## 1. Executive summary
Interactive AI Childhood proposes a caregiver-supervised audiovisual learning platform that turns stories into two-way conversations. Characters ask questions, listen to responses, react within safety constraints, and let children's choices influence later scenes. Caregivers receive privacy-conscious activity summaries and suggested offline activities.

**Status:** concept only; no implemented product or original experimental results.

## 2. Problem and motivation
Children's videos sometimes invite answers without actually listening. Response-contingent interaction may support active participation and language practice. It should **not** be assumed that screen exposure inherently causes communication deficits or that AI conversation replaces human relationships. Outcomes depend on age, content, family context and design.

## 3. Goals
- Practice conversational turn-taking, asking questions and verbal expression.
- Support vocabulary and listening comprehension, including English-language learning.
- Explore empathy, cooperation, boundaries and conflict resolution through stories.
- Encourage transfer of learning into activities with family, teachers and peers.
- Inform caregivers without creating excessive surveillance.

## 4. Child experience
1. A caregiver creates and configures a profile and age-appropriate goals.
2. The child starts a story made from original or properly licensed scenes.
3. A character asks a question or presents a choice and playback pauses.
4. The system interprets a spoken response (or accessible alternative) and selects a constrained reply.
5. Choices affect story branches and, with permission, limited continuity between sessions.
6. The session ends naturally with an invitation to try a skill offline.

### Illustrative narrative
**Day 1:** a character asks for help finding an object; the child chooses a path and explains why. **Day 5:** the character recalls an allowed fictional preference and invites the child to help an animal. **Day 12:** two characters disagree and the child suggests a solution. These are fictional examples, not a validated curriculum.

## 5. Two possible formats
**A. Dedicated platform:** original/licensed story library, branching plots, controlled characters and dialogue. Best initial MVP for editorial and safety control.

**B. Interactive layer over existing videos:** detect pause points, ask questions and respond to authorized third-party content **only with appropriate rights and in compliance with platform terms**. Do not assume permission to reuse YouTube videos or characters such as Dora.

## 6. Story continuity and character relationships
Characters may remember limited story choices (e.g. a fictional pet) but must not encourage exclusivity, secrecy from caregivers, emotional dependence or pressure to return. They should be clearly identified as artificial characters and encourage real-world relationships.

## 7. Caregiver dashboard
Possible features: practiced topics, session duration, target vocabulary, short response examples (with specific consent), content preferences, time limits, data review and deletion. Avoid personality scores, diagnoses, or claims of psychological progress from usage data alone. Distinguish **observed in-app activity** from **skills demonstrated in real life**.

## 8. Educational design
Use session goals, gradual difficulty, non-punitive feedback, open-ended questions, accessible communication options, and co-design with educators, speech-language professionals and child-development experts. Include shared adult-child experiences and appropriate breaks.

## 9. Evaluation and research
**Hypothesis:** compared with passive or pseudo-interactive video, a safe conversational experience may improve specific engagement and learning measures; this must be tested.

With appropriate consent and ethics review, compare groups using vocabulary comprehension, response variety, conversational turn quality, delayed retention and transfer to human conversations. Track adverse outcomes, frustration, speech-recognition bias and excessive use. More responses do not automatically establish better social skills.

## 10. Minimum viable prototype
One original 5–10 minute story, 2–3 branches, limited intent recognition, optional speech input, human-reviewed replies, basic caregiver controls, minimal summaries and deletion tools. Avoid unrestricted dialogue and fully generated video at first.

## 11. Risks and constraints
Children's privacy, verifiable caregiver consent, voice processing, unpredictable responses, unsuitable content, parasocial dependency, language/accent bias, accessibility, copyright and misleading developmental conclusions. See [safety](../SAFETY.md).
